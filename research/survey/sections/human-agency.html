Human agency, personalization, and proactive assistance

Computer use becomes a human–agent interaction problem when a system chooses when to intervene, interprets personal context, or changes information on a user's behalf. A useful distinction is between recognizing a likely intention, obtaining authority to act on it, executing the operation correctly, and explaining the resulting state. These requirements can vary independently. We use recent interface-specific studies to clarify the distinction, rather than treating more autonomous behavior as a universally better outcome.

Act2Intention separates intention understanding, prediction, and execution. Its primary text specifies user-confirmed execution, and its data construction separates observed trajectories from trajectories generated using behavior-derived or generated personas. The limitations explicitly distinguish predicting an intention from choosing an appropriate moment to interrupt. They also state that real-user acceptance of proactive suggestions was not evaluated. Thus, its intention metrics support the proposed modeling tasks; they do not establish that unsolicited assistance is welcome or that synthetic personas represent a deployment population.

ProactiveVA studies assistance inside visual-analytics workflows. It combines interaction-log interpretation with suggestions, previews, and interface operations. Its in-lab evaluation involved ten users and reported feedback on assistance and control; the discussion records requests for finer intervention during the agent's process. These observations motivate studying the timing and controllability of assistance. They should be read as evidence from the reported visual-analytics setting, not as a population-wide estimate of the benefit of proactive agents.

NeXUI makes explanation and user oversight part of an assistive-interface evaluation. Its packaged tasks use recorded linear transition graphs; unsupported actions do not create alternative paths. The reported model experiments used a 25-task validation split and a text-based baseline, without screenshots supplied to the model. This is a controlled setting for studying action–explanation alignment and confirmation boundaries. Its result should not be interpreted as a measurement of live open-web performance or a user study demonstrating accessibility benefits.

A research agenda follows from these distinctions. Personalization experiments should separate recall of an explicit preference from inference about an unspoken preference, and should report what happens when the two conflict. Proactive systems should expose how users reject, correct, or revoke a proposal. Explanations should be checked against observable state changes rather than judged only for fluency. These are evaluation recommendations of this survey. A suitable study would measure task success, correction effort, unwanted actions, interruption burden, and perceived control together, with consent and data minimization appropriate to the setting.
